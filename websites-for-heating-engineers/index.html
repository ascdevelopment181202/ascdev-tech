What does a heating engineer's website have to do?

Heating work comes in at three different moments. Each one needs its own page, because the customer is asking a different question each time.

The momentWhat the customer is askingWhat the page has to show
The boiler has stoppedCan someone come today, and do they work on my boiler?A tap-to-call number, the fuels and makes you repair, your area and your real hours.
The boiler needs replacingWho do I trust with a big job, and what will the quote include?Photos of your own installs, the brands you fit, your registrations and how the quote works.
The service or safety check is dueCan I get this booked without a phone call?A short booking form, what the visit covers and the paperwork the customer gets.

A single "services" page cannot answer all three. The breakdown customer may leave if the number is buried, and the replacement customer has nothing to judge you by.

What pages should a heating engineer's website have?

Build the menu around the heating work you want more of. These are the pages we plan for first:

  • Boiler installation and replacement: combi, system and regular boilers, what a swap involves and what happens to the old one.
  • Boiler breakdown and repair: the faults you see, such as a boiler losing pressure or locking out, and how fast you can attend.
  • Boiler servicing: what you check, how long the visit takes and how to book.
  • Landlord gas safety checks: written for landlords and letting agents, with a way to book more than one property.
  • Oil and LPG heating, if you work off the gas grid.
  • Heat pumps, if you install or service them.
  • Radiators, controls and system cleaning: smart thermostats, zone valves, power flushing and filters.
  • Underfloor heating and hot water cylinders, if you take them on.
  • Area pages for the towns you want work in.

Leave out anything you do not do. A page for work you turn down only brings calls you have to refuse.

Which registrations should a heating engineer show online?

Customers can look a heating firm up. The first three bodies below each run a public search, so make that check easy to pass.

  • Gas Safe Register. It calls itself "the official list of businesses that are legally permitted to carry out gas work". Its check the register page lets anyone search by business registration number or trading name, or by an engineer's licence number.
  • OFTEC, for oil. It runs a competent persons scheme for technicians who install and maintain oil, solid fuel and renewable heating equipment, with a public find a technician search.
  • MCS, for heat pumps and other renewables. It has a find an installer tool that shows whether a business is certified.
  • Manufacturer accreditations, if you hold them.

Show the registration number next to the logo, and use the same trading name as on the register, so a customer who searches finds a match. Gas Safe also tells the public that not all engineers are qualified for every type of gas work. So list the gas work you are qualified to do, not just the badge.

How should a heating website handle boiler quotes?

A new boiler is a big job, and a customer may ask more than one firm for a price. So the quote page should be easy to start and clear about what happens next.

A good quote form asks for the few details you need to give a sensible first answer:

  • The fuel: mains gas, oil or LPG.
  • The boiler they have now, and where it sits in the house.
  • The number of bathrooms and radiators.
  • A photo of the boiler and of the flue outside.
  • Their postcode and the best time to ring.

Then say what your quote includes, such as the flush, the filter, the controls, the removal of the old boiler and the warranty you register. Say whether you visit before quoting. Two firms can quote for the same house and mean different jobs, so spelling out what is in yours helps the customer compare fairly.

How does the website bring customers back every year?

Some heating work repeats. Customers who have their boiler serviced book again, and a landlord's safety check comes round every year. The Health and Safety Executive tells landlords that a gas safety check must be done every year on each gas appliance and flue, and that existing tenants must get a copy of the record within 28 days of the check.

So the site needs two pages built for coming back, not for first contact. A servicing page with a short booking form gives last year's customer a quick way to rebook. A landlord page explains the check, the record you hand over and how to book several properties at once. If you send your own service reminders by text or email, point them at these pages.

A customer who comes back each year is also a good person to ask for a review. Reviews are part of every ASC plan.

Should a heating engineer's website cover heat pumps?

If you fit them, yes, on a page of their own. The government's Boiler Upgrade Scheme is open to people in England and Wales and gives grants towards heat pumps and biomass boilers. Its how to apply page tells homeowners to contact MCS certified installers for quotes, and says the installer applies for the grant on the customer's behalf.

So a homeowner who wants the grant has to start with an installer. A heat pump page should say whether you are MCS certified and registered for the scheme, that you make the application, which systems you fit and what you need to know about the property. The MCS search can be filtered to installers registered for the scheme, so customers can check.

If you do not fit heat pumps, do not add the page. Say what you do instead.

One site, planned around the work you want more of. If boilers and heating bring in the better jobs, they lead the menu and plumbing sits behind them. If it is the other way round, our websites for plumbers page describes that layout. You do not need two websites.

Should I list the boiler brands I fit?

Yes. Name the brands you install and service, and say so if you are an accredited installer for any of them. A customer who already has a make of boiler in mind can then see straight away that you work on it. Only list brands you really fit.

I only work on oil boilers. Will a website still help off the gas grid?

Yes. Say plainly that you work on oil, show your OFTEC registration if you hold one, and name the villages and rural areas you cover. A clear oil page stops calls for gas work you do not take on, and gives search engines and AI assistants a plain statement of what you do.

I am not MCS certified. Should my site still mention heat pumps?

Only say what you really do. If you service heat pumps or fit the pipework and cylinders for another installer, say that. Do not suggest you can arrange the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant unless you are set up to apply for it, because customers can look installers up on the MCS website.

How is a builder's website different from other trade websites?

Nobody hires a builder in a hurry. An extension or a loft conversion is one of the biggest things a household spends money on. People read, compare and think for weeks before they ask for a quote, and they often come back to the same sites several times.

So the site does a different job from a plumber's or an electrician's. It is not there to catch an urgent call. It is there to show what you have built, explain how you work and make a careful customer feel safe enough to get in touch.

That changes what goes on it. Projects come first, the process comes second, and the contact form asks enough questions to tell you whether the job is worth a visit.

What should a builder's project pages show?

Your past work is the main thing you are selling. A gallery of photos with no words wastes it. Each project should be a short story a customer can picture themselves in.

What pages should a building firm's website have?

  • A page for each type of work you want: house extensions, loft conversions, renovations, new builds, garage conversions.
  • Project pages, as above, linked from the matching service page.
  • How we work: the stages from first visit to handover, who handles drawings and approvals, and how payments are staged.
  • About: the people, how long you have traded and the memberships you hold, such as the FMB or TrustMark.
  • Area pages for the main towns you build in.
  • Contact, with a proper enquiry form.

The "how we work" page is easy to leave out, and it is the one a nervous customer reads most closely. A clear process on paper suggests a clear process on site.

How should a builder's website handle enquiries?

A builder does not need more enquiries. A builder needs the right ones. A form that only asks for a name and number brings calls about jobs that are too small, too far away or a year from starting.

A better form asks a few simple questions:

  • What kind of project is it?
  • Where is the property?
  • Do you have drawings or planning permission yet?
  • When would you like the work to start?

That lets you sort the serious from the early browsers before you drive anywhere. People who are not ready yet can still be sent something useful, such as your guide to how a project runs, so they remember you when they are.

Which searches should a builder's website be built around?

  • "House extension builders" plus your town
  • "Loft conversion" plus your town
  • "Builders near me" and "local builders"
  • Renovation and refurbishment searches
  • Planning-stage questions, such as how long an extension takes or whether a loft needs planning permission

Those last ones matter more for builders than for most trades. Customers ask them months before they hire anyone, and the firm that answers well is the one they remember.

How many projects do I need to show on a building website?

Three or four well-documented projects are enough to start. Each should have before, during and after photos and a few lines on what the customer wanted and how long it took. We add more as you finish new jobs.

I am a general builder. Should the site focus on one type of work?

Lead with the work you most want, and give it the most space. A general builder can still list everything they take on, but a site that tries to push every service equally tends to rank for none of them. We help you choose the two or three to put first.

Do customers really read a "how we work" page?

The serious ones do. Someone about to spend a large sum wants to know what happens after they say yes: who draws the plans, how approvals are handled, how payments are staged and who to ring with a problem. Answering that up front removes a common reason people hold back.

My work comes from recommendations. Do I still need a website?

Yes, because recommended customers still look you up before they call. A site with real projects confirms what their friend told them. It also brings enquiries from people outside your current circle, which matters when a quiet spell comes.

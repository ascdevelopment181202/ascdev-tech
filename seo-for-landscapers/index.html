What does search engine optimisation mean for a landscaping firm?

Search engine optimisation, or SEO, is the work of making your firm easy to find and easy to understand, for search engines and for the people using them.

Landscaping is a wide trade. One firm can lay patios, build fences, fit decking and turf lawns. A customer with one job in mind can search for that job alone.

So landscaping SEO is mostly a matter of matching. Each kind of work gets its own page, its own photos and its own place on your Google profile. A search for one job then finds the part of your business that does it.

How do project photos help a landscaper get found?

Project photos are a large part of a landscaper's proof. For search, each one needs words with it. Google's image guidance sets out what helps:

  • A plain filename. Google asks for filenames that are short but descriptive. "sandstone-patio-leeds.jpg" says more than "IMG_4021.jpg".
  • Alt text. Google calls it the most important attribute for telling it about an image, and says not to fill it with keywords.
  • Text beside the photo. Google says to place images near relevant text, on a page about the same subject.
  • A normal image on the page. Google says standard HTML image elements help its crawlers, and that it does not index images set in CSS.

The same photos belong on your Google Business Profile. Google's photo guidelines ask for pictures that are in focus and well lit, with no heavy filters. In its words, the image should represent reality.

Which planning questions should a landscaper's website answer?

Before they ask for a quote, a customer may want to know if the job needs permission. The Planning Portal publishes guidance for England, and a landscaper's site can link to it.

  • Front garden paving. Its paving guidance says a driveway of any size needs no permission if the surface is permeable, or if rainwater drains to a lawn or border. Above five square metres, traditional impermeable paving needs permission.
  • Fences, walls and gates. Its fencing guidance gives height limits of one metre next to a road used by vehicles and two metres elsewhere. Listed buildings are treated differently.
  • Decking. Its decking guidance says decking needs no permission if it is no more than 30cm above the ground and, with other extensions and outbuildings, covers no more than half the garden.

State the rule in a line, link to the source and say how you handle it on a job. Do not promise an outcome. The limits have other conditions, so send the customer to the full page.

How does a landscaper show up on Google Maps?

Google's local ranking page says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and popularity. It says complete and accurate profiles are more likely to show, and that more reviews and positive ratings can help. It also says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.

For a landscaping firm, the profile work looks like this:

  • List each kind of work as its own service: paving, driveways, fencing, decking, turfing.
  • Add photos job by job, so the profile shows the range of what you build.
  • Set the areas you travel to and keep them honest.
  • Ask for a review when the garden is handed over. Google lets you share a link or a QR code, and prohibits offering anything in return.

Do BALI, APL and waste carrier listings help a landscaper?

They give a customer a place to check you that is not your own website. Each is also a page about your firm on another site.

  • BALI. BALI has a public directory of accredited members. It can be refined by domestic or commercial work and by type of work.
  • APL. The Association of Professional Landscapers has a member directory searched by postcode, county and service. It says its members are inspected regularly and accredited by TrustMark.
  • Waste carrier registration. In England, GOV.UK says a business that transports waste must register, and that registered firms appear in a public register. The other nations have their own process.

Google says popularity is based partly on how many websites link to your business. Nobody outside Google can say what one listing is worth, so treat these as proof for customers first. Show only what you hold. Our guide to trade registers lists more.

Questions landscapers ask about SEO

How long does SEO take for a landscaper?

Plan for three to six months for results to build. Fixes to your Google Business Profile and your website can show sooner. If you want the enquiries for a particular season, count back from it and start early enough to allow for that wait.

Should a landscaper have one page for all their work or a page for each type?

A page for each type of work you want more of. A patio search and a fencing search are different searches, and one general page has to answer both at once. Start with the work that matters most to you, give it its own photos and its own words, then add the next.

Can I use the photos I already post on social media?

Yes, if they are your own jobs. Put them on your website as well, next to a few lines about the job. Google says images should sit near relevant text, with a short descriptive filename and alt text. That gives Google something to read about each photo.

I work in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland. Do the planning figures on this page apply?

No. The figures on this page come from the Planning Portal's guidance, which covers England, and the Planning Portal says the policy in Wales may differ. If you work outside England, link to your own nation's guidance on your answer pages and say which nations you cover.

I am already booked up for months. Is SEO still worth doing?

It can be. Being found is not only about more enquiries. It lets you choose them. You can build out the pages for the work you would like more of, and leave the rest. Nobody can promise a result, so judge it against the kind of job you want to be asked for.
